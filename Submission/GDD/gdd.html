1. Overview

Sprint or Shift is a first-person maze game made in Unity 6. You are in a dark neon maze and you have to reach a glowing portal at the end. The catch is that if you stand still for two seconds you get sent back to the start, and some of the walls move when you are not looking at them. So you can't stop to think and you can't go back the way you came.

The question we wanted to answer with the prototype was whether two simple rules, "don't stop" and "don't look back", are enough to make a normal maze feel tense and make players commit to their choices.

GenreFirst-person maze / action puzzle
PlatformPC with keyboard and mouse (we built it for macOS)
EngineUnity 6 with the Universal Render Pipeline
Play timeAbout 3 to 8 minutes for both levels
Target playersAges 12 and up, casual and regular players

Design goals

We kept a few goals in mind while making decisions:

  • Moving should always be better than waiting. There is no pause, no map and no slow careful option.
  • The maze should react to where you look. Walls only change behind your back.
  • The rules should be simple enough to write on a sign. The chaos should come from the rules mixing together, not from randomness.
  • Two good levels are better than a big unfinished game.

In terms of how the player should feel, we wanted them to panic a little when they stop, to be nervous about turning around, and eventually to realise that standing still can also be used on purpose to get out of a dead end.

2. Rules and controls

Rule 1: Don't stop

If the player moves slower than 0.1 m/s for 2 seconds, they are teleported back to the start. A red overlay with a countdown starts fading in after about 0.3 seconds as a warning. The timer only starts after the first step, so the player can look around at the start without being punished.

Rule 2: Don't look back

The purple walls only change after the player has seen them (on screen with nothing blocking the view) and then turned so they are behind them (more than 110 degrees away from where they are looking). There are two kinds: one-way gates close after you walk through them, and toggle walls switch between open and closed each time.

Rule 3: Dead ends are traps

Some paths lead into dead ends on purpose. If you get stuck, you can stand still for 2 seconds to go back to the start, and all the purple walls go back to how they were, so the level can always be finished.

Winning

Walking into the cyan portal finishes the level. Level 1 leads to Level 2, and finishing Level 2 shows the victory screen, where R restarts the game.

KeyAction
W A S D or arrow keysMove (up to 6.5 m/s)
MouseLook around
EscRelease the mouse
Left clickCapture the mouse again

3. Core loop

Moment to moment, the player is running and picking directions at junctions with the two-second limit always in the background. Over a few minutes, every reset costs time but teaches them a bit more of the maze and where the traps are. Across the whole game, Level 1 introduces each rule separately and Level 2 puts them all together with faster walls.

4. Mechanics

4.1 When walls move

To count as seen, a wall has to be inside the camera view and there has to be a clear line from the camera to the wall. This means walls hidden behind other walls don't count.

Wall typeStartsMoves whenWhy we added it
One-way gateOpenThe player has seen it, walked at least 3 m past it, and it is now behind them. Only happens once.Stops the player going back, so a wrong choice becomes a dead end.
Toggle wallOpenThe player has seen it and it is now behind them. Happens every time, with a 2 second cooldown in Level 1 and 0.6 seconds in Level 2.Changes the routes by opening shortcuts or closing off areas.

A wall will never move if the player is within 1.5 m of it, so it can't close on top of them. All walls reset when the player is teleported.

4.2 Tuning the stillness rule

SettingValueReason
Time limit2 secondsEnough time to turn a corner and read a sign, but not enough to stop and study the maze.
Speed threshold0.1 m/sIt uses the player's real speed, so walking into a wall counts as standing still. This is what makes Rule 3 work.
Warning overlay0.3 s to 1.2 sShort pauses don't trigger it, but it is very obvious before the teleport happens.

5. Level design

Both levels are made of 4 m grid cells. We wrote the layouts as text in the level builder script (SprintOrShiftLevelBuilder.cs), so changing the maze is just a case of editing characters. We also checked both layouts with a small search program to make sure the exit can be reached even if every toggle wall is closed.

Level 1: Tutorial

Level 1 is one long winding corridor (33 cells to the end) with two one-way gates and three signs. There are no branches, so the player can learn the rules without getting lost. The first thing they see is the "DON'T STOP" sign. "DON'T LOOK BACK" is just before the first gate, which closes behind them a few seconds later, and "KEEP MOVING" comes before the second gate and the portal.

Level 2: High Chaos

Level 2 is 17 by 13 cells with a shortest route of 41 cells. It has five one-way gates, two fast toggle walls and four dead ends. A few parts were designed to trick the player:

  • The corridor with the "DEAD END" sign leads towards the middle and stops one wall away from the real path, which is behind a gate. The idea is to teach players not to trust where a corridor looks like it goes.
  • Next to the "TRUST NOTHING" sign there is a shortcut towards the exit that closes once it is behind you. If you walk away from it you have to go the long way.
  • On the left there is a big open area that looks inviting but is full of dead ends, and the toggle wall you enter through can close behind you.

6. Visual style and feedback

We went for a dark neon look. Everything is made from Unity's basic cubes with flat colours: dark blue walls, an almost black floor and dark fog. Anything the player needs to notice glows, like the green start pad, the signs and the cyan portal. The moving walls are purple so that players who pay attention can tell them apart from normal walls. There is also a flashlight on the camera, which limits how far you can see and adds to the tension.

What happensWhat the player sees
Standing stillThe screen turns red with "DON'T STOP!" and a countdown
Teleported"TOO SLOW! BACK TO START" and they appear on the green start pad
Wall movesNothing at the time, since it is always behind you. You only find out when you turn around. We want to add a sound for this.
Level starts or endsA large title in the middle of the screen

7. MDA analysis

The feelings we are mainly going for are sensation (urgency) and challenge, with a bit of fantasy from the idea of being hunted by a maze that is alive. The table shows three chains from mechanic to dynamic to aesthetic. More detail is in the separate MDA document.

MechanicDynamicAesthetic
1Moving slower than 0.1 for 2 seconds sends you back to the start, with a countdown warning.Players don't stop at junctions and choose quickly. Some wiggle on the spot to buy time, which doesn't work.Urgency and stress (sensation, challenge)
2A wall is armed once you have seen it, and moves when it is more than 110 degrees behind you.Players avoid turning around, glance back nervously and stare at walls so they can't move.Paranoia (sensation, fantasy)
3Dead ends and one-way gates, plus the reset when you stand still (which also resets every wall).When trapped, players learn to stand still on purpose to get out.Tension and then relief (challenge, discovery)

The chains depend on each other. Because you can't stop (1), you can't keep an eye on the walls behind you, which is why the moving walls are dangerous (2). The walls closing behind you create real dead ends (3). And the way out of a dead end is the stillness rule from (1), so the same rule punishes you for hesitating and saves you when you're stuck.

The dynamics in the table are what we expect players to do. We still need to confirm them with the external playtest. Chain 2 has already changed once, because in early testing the walls felt random rather than scary (see section 10).

8. Design principles

PrincipleHow we used it
Teaching through level designLevel 1 has one path and each sign is right before the thing it explains. Level 2 only uses rules the player already knows.
FeedbackThe red overlay shows a countdown, every teleport shows a message and important things glow.
SignifiersMoving walls are purple and normal walls are dark blue. The glowing portal shows where to go.
Difficulty curveLevel 1 has 2 gates and no branches. Level 2 has 5 gates, 2 toggle walls that reset three times faster, 4 dead ends and a trick corridor.
Risk and rewardThe shortcut in Level 2 saves time but can close behind you.
FairnessThe walls always follow the same rule and reset when you are teleported, and the exit can always be reached.

9. Technical implementation

We used Unity 6 (6000.4.0f1) with URP, the new Input System and TextMeshPro. The game runs on three main scripts. The player and the walls both talk to the GameManager, but the GameManager doesn't depend on them.

ScriptWhat it does
FirstPersonControllerMovement with a CharacterController, mouse look, the 2 second stillness timer, teleporting back to the start and detecting the portal.
DynamicMazeWallEvery frame it checks if the wall is on camera and not blocked (using a Physics.Linecast), whether it is behind the player, and the cooldown and distance. When it moves it turns the wall's renderer and collider on or off.
GameManagerKeeps track of the start point, shows the warning overlay and messages, resets the walls after a teleport, loads the next level and shows the victory screen.
SprintOrShiftLevelBuilder (editor only)Builds both levels from the text layouts, including walls, signs, the player, UI and lighting.

We first tried Unity's OnBecameInvisible() to detect when the player wasn't looking at a wall, but it also counts the Scene view camera in the editor, stops working once the wall's renderer is turned off, and doesn't know if something is in the way. Checking the player's camera ourselves turned out to be much more reliable.

Both scenes have the same hierarchy: Directional Light, Global Volume, GameManager, Environment (floor and start point), Maze (walls, dynamic walls and signs), Player with the camera and flashlight, UI (crosshair, overlay, messages and victory panel) and the FinishPortal.

10. Playtesting

Internal playtest

We played both levels in the editor ourselves, and Adel also got his brother, who hadn't seen the game before, to play it on his laptop.

ProblemWhat we changed
The pink debug boxes at the dead ends in Level 2 were always visible.They were editor gizmos showing in the game view. Now they only show when a wall is selected in the editor.
The mouse was too slow.Sensitivity went from 0.12 to 0.45 and smoothing from 20 to 30.
Adel's brother found that walls were closing even when they weren't in his line of sight, so they seemed random.Walls now need a clear line of sight and have to be behind the player before they move, and they all start open. This was the most important fix, because before it the walls were confusing instead of scary.

Rule checks

After fixing these we tested each rule in play mode: the stillness teleport, gates closing behind you, walls resetting after a teleport, hidden walls not counting as seen, turning 90 degrees not triggering a wall while turning fully around does, and the portal loading Level 2. We also confirmed both mazes can be finished.

External playtest

We haven't done this yet. The plan is to have 3 to 5 people who haven't played before try it for about 10 minutes each, only telling them the controls. We will count how often they pause at junctions, turn around and use the reset on purpose, then ask them five short questions. The full plan is in the Playtesting Notes document.
